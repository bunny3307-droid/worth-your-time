MIAMI · EAT

Cuban food, stone crab, and the meals worth planning around.

Miami's food scene is deep enough to build an entire trip around. Here is how to navigate it by what you are actually craving, not just what is trending.

Cuban food, done right

Little Havana is the center of gravity: cafecito served through walk-up ventanita windows, croquetas, medianoche sandwiches and ropa vieja. Versailles Restaurant, open since the 1970s, is the classic, high-volume stop for a full Cuban menu and the neighborhood's unofficial living room—expect it to be busy and loud, which is part of the point. For a quicker taste, a ventanita coffee window and a pastelito is a five-minute, five-dollar introduction to the whole cuisine.

Stone crab season

Stone crab is Miami's signature seafood, in season roughly mid-October through early May. Joe's Stone Crab, operating since 1913 in South Beach, is the institution most people mean when they talk about it—expect a wait and a splurge-level bill, but it is a genuinely singular Miami experience rather than a tourist trap. Outside of stone crab season, most seafood houses shift the menu to other local catch, so check before building a trip around it.

Wynwood and the food-hall scene

Wynwood's warehouses have turned into some of the city's best food halls—multiple vendors under one roof, good for groups with different cravings and for grazing rather than committing to one restaurant. Pair a food hall with a Wynwood Walls visit for an easy afternoon-into-evening combination.

Brickell and Design District dining

This is where Miami's more polished, reservation-worthy restaurants cluster: Latin fusion, upscale seafood and a fair amount of see-and-be-seen energy. It is a good fit for a date night or a splurge dinner, less so for a casual weeknight meal.

Brunch culture

Miami takes brunch seriously, especially in the Design District and along the beach—expect bottomless-mimosa deals and lines at the best spots on weekends. If brunch matters to your trip, book ahead or go early.

Ventanita coffee + pastelito, Little Havana

THE WORTH-IT RULE: A restaurant that requires forty-five minutes each way is competing with every restaurant closer to where you already are. Factor the drive into the decision, not just the menu.
