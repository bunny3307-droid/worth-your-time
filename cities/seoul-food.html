SEOUL · EAT

Korean BBQ, street markets and what to actually order.

Seoul's food scene rewards a bit of structure. Here is how to navigate it by what you are actually craving.

Korean BBQ, done properly

Korean BBQ is a social meal, not a quick one—budget at least ninety minutes, order in a group, and let the banchan (side dishes) keep arriving. Most places grill at the table; staff will often help with timing if you ask.

Gwangjang Market

One of Seoul's oldest and most famous traditional markets, and the single best introduction to Korean street food in one place: bindaetteok (mung bean pancakes), mayak gimbap (small seaweed rice rolls) and a long row of stalls selling everything from raw fish to knife-cut noodles. Go hungry and go with cash on hand.

Street food staples

Tteokbokki (spicy rice cakes), hotteok (sweet filled pancakes) and Myeongdong's street food alley are the fastest way to eat well for very little money between bigger meals.

Korean-Chinese food

Jjajangmyeon (black bean noodles) and jjamppong (spicy seafood noodle soup) are a distinct Korean-Chinese fusion cuisine with its own dedicated restaurants—worth seeking out as a category of its own, not a substitute for Chinese food elsewhere.

Local food streets

Mangwon and Yeonnam-dong, both near Hongdae, have quieter, more residential food streets that skew toward locals rather than tourists—a good contrast to Myeongdong if you want a calmer meal.

Dining etiquette

Meals are shared, banchan is often free and refillable, and tipping is not expected or practiced in Korea. Pouring drinks for others rather than yourself is a small, appreciated custom at a group meal.

ONE-STOP INTRO

Gwangjang Market

GROUP DINNER

Korean BBQ, book ahead on weekends

QUICK & CHEAP

Tteokbokki or hotteok, any street stall

THE WORTH-IT RULE: No tipping, shared dishes and refillable banchan mean Seoul dining is often cheaper per person than it looks on the menu—factor that in before ruling out a nicer restaurant.
